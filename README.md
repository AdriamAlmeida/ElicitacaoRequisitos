# Requirements Elicitation

## Requirements Engineering

Requirements Engineering is the systematic process responsible for discovering, analyzing, documenting, and verifying the requirements of a system. This process examines stakeholders' needs, the context of the work, and the constraints that guide how the solution will be built by the development team.

It acts as a bridge between two fundamental sides of any software project: on one side, the **stakeholders**, who see the problem from a business perspective; on the other, the **development team**, which needs to turn that problem into a functional technical solution.

### Stakeholders

A stakeholder is any person or group with an interest in the system, or affected by it. Stakeholders are the ones who bring the business needs, usage scenarios, and constraints that the project must respect. They don't think in terms of code or architecture, but in terms of real problems that need to be solved.

### Development Team

The development team needs information clear enough to design and build the system. When this information is poorly gathered or communicated, the team ends up building something technically correct, but that solves the wrong problem — one of the most common and most expensive risks in software projects.

It is precisely to avoid this kind of failure that the requirements elicitation stage exists and is treated with so much importance within Requirements Engineering.

---

## What is Requirements Elicitation

Elicitation is the first, and one of the most critical, stages of Requirements Engineering. It is the process of discovering the stakeholders' real needs, rather than simply collecting them passively.

The term "elicit" is deliberately used instead of "gather" or "collect" because, in practice, stakeholders themselves often cannot clearly articulate what they need. It's up to the person responsible for elicitation — an analyst, a PO, or a requirements engineer — to dig deeper, ask the right questions, observe the work context, and interpret implicit information in order to extract requirements that are not yet explicit.

Requirement errors identified only in the final stages of a project are significantly more expensive to fix than errors identified early on. Because of this, poor elicitation tends to generate rework, schedule delays, and customer dissatisfaction, since the rest of the process — analysis, specification, validation, and requirements management — depends directly on the quality of this first stage.

## Requirements Elicitation Techniques

There are several techniques used to elicit requirements, and they are usually combined with one another, since no single technique is capable of covering every scenario:

- **Interviews**, conducted individually or in groups with stakeholders.
- **Questionnaires**, useful when there are many geographically distributed stakeholders.
- **On-site observation**, following the user while they perform their real day-to-day tasks.
- **Brainstorming**, for collective idea generation and identification of needs.
- **Requirements workshops**, structured sessions bringing multiple stakeholders together.
- **Prototypes**, used to validate understanding through visual representations of the system.
- **Document analysis**, including manuals, internal processes, and legacy systems.
- **User Stories**, an agile and simplified format for capturing a need from the user's perspective.

---

## Elicitation vs. Requirements Analysis

These two concepts are often confused, but they represent distinct stages of the process. Elicitation focuses on discovering what stakeholders actually need, working as a collection and investigation stage, and as a result produces "raw," unrefined requirements.

Requirements analysis, on the other hand, focuses on organizing, refining, and prioritizing everything discovered during elicitation, working on the understanding and structuring of the information gathered. The outcome of this stage is clear, testable, and unambiguous requirements, ready to guide development.

---

## Best Practices in Requirements Elicitation

Some practices help make elicitation more effective and reliable:

- Never assume you already know what the stakeholder wants — always ask and confirm.
- Document elicited requirements as early as possible, to avoid losing important information.
- Combine multiple elicitation techniques, since each one covers different aspects of the problem.
- Continuously validate understanding with the stakeholder, maintaining an ongoing feedback loop.
- Treat ambiguities and implicit requirements as warning signs, not as details that can be ignored.
